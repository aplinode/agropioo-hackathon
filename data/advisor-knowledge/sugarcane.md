# Sugarcane Farming Guide — Pakistan

## Overview

Sugarcane is one of the most important cash crops in Pakistan. It is grown as both a Kharif (spring-planted) and ratoon crop, primarily in Punjab and Sindh provinces. Sugarcane provides raw material for the sugar industry and is also used for gur (jaggery) production at the village level.

Pakistan ranks among the top sugarcane-producing countries globally. The crop occupies approximately 1 million hectares across the country, with Punjab accounting for about two-thirds of total production and Sindh contributing most of the remainder.

### Recommended Varieties

**Punjab:**
- **HSF-240** — High-yielding, good sugar recovery, moderately resistant to red rot and smut. Suitable for central and northern Punjab.
- **SPF-234** — Early maturing, high sugar content, tolerant to water stress. Performs well in sandy loam soils.
- **CPF-246** — Thick-stalked, high tonnage variety with good ratoonability. Resistant to lodging under normal conditions.

**Sindh:**
- **HSF-240** and **SPF-234** are also widely grown in Sindh.
- **Thatta-10**, **BL-4**, and **S-2003-US-633** are additional varieties recommended by Sindh agriculture research stations.

Always obtain certified seed from approved sources such as the Punjab Seed Corporation or Sindh Seed Corporation. Certified seed reduces disease carryover and improves germination uniformity.

## Crop Calendar

### Planting Seasons

| Season | Planting Window | Notes |
|--------|----------------|-------|
| Spring (Kharif) | February–March | Main planting season in Punjab and Sindh. Soil temperature should be at least 20°C. |
| Autumn | September–October | Secondary planting window. Risk of poor establishment if winter arrives early. More common in Sindh where winters are milder. |

Spring-planted cane generally yields higher than autumn-planted cane because it benefits from the full growing season through summer.

### Growth Stages

1. **Germination (Weeks 1–4):** Buds on planted setts sprout. Soil moisture must be adequate but not waterlogged. This is the most vulnerable stage.
2. **Tillering (Months 2–4):** Primary shoots produce secondary tillers. Adequate nitrogen and moisture are critical. Final stalk population is determined during this phase.
3. **Grand Growth / Elongation (Months 4–8):** Rapid cane elongation. Peak water and nutrient demand. This stage coincides with the monsoon season in most regions.
4. **Maturation / Sugar Accumulation (Months 8–12):** Growth slows. Sucrose accumulates in the stalk. Reduce irrigation frequency. Cool nights and warm days favor sugar buildup.
5. **Harvest maturity:** Typically 10–12 months after planting for plant cane; 9–11 months for ratoon crops.

### Harvest Window

Harvest runs from **November through March**. Mills typically open in November and close by April. Early-harvested cane (November–December) may have lower sugar recovery but avoids peak-season congestion at mills. Late harvest (February–March) risks sucrose inversion and weight loss as temperatures rise.

## Irrigation

Sugarcane is a water-intensive crop. Total water requirement ranges from **1,500 to 2,000 mm** over the growing season depending on soil type, climate, and variety.

### Irrigation Schedule

| Growth Stage | Frequency | Notes |
|-------------|-----------|-------|
| Germination (first month) | Every 7–10 days | Light, frequent irrigation to keep soil moist. Avoid flooding that causes sett rot. |
| Tillering (months 2–4) | Every 10–12 days | Consistent moisture supports tiller production. |
| Grand Growth (months 4–8) | Every 7–10 days in summer; every 10–15 days otherwise | Peak demand period. Water stress during grand growth permanently reduces yield. In June–July, weekly irrigation may be needed. |
| Maturation (months 8–12) | Every 15–20 days | Gradually reduce frequency. Stop irrigation 2–3 weeks before harvest to promote sugar concentration. |

### Important Irrigation Notes

- Furrow irrigation is the most common method. Ensure furrows are properly graded to avoid waterlogging at the tail end.
- Drip irrigation can save 30–40% water compared to flood/furrow methods and is increasingly promoted under government subsidy schemes.
- Waterlogging is as harmful as drought. Standing water for more than 48 hours causes root rot and reduces yield.
- Saline groundwater (EC > 1.5 dS/m) should be avoided or blended with canal water. Sugarcane is moderately salt-tolerant but sensitive during germination.

**Safety warning:** Never irrigate during midday heat in summer (11 AM–3 PM). Evaporation losses are highest and workers risk heat exhaustion.

## Fertilizer Management

Sugarcane is a heavy feeder. Nutrient removal per acre of a 40-tonne crop is approximately 80 kg N, 30 kg P₂O₅, and 100 kg K₂O. Actual fertilizer rates should be based on soil test results from an accredited laboratory.

### General Fertilizer Schedule (per acre)

| Timing | Fertilizer | Rate (per acre) | Purpose |
|--------|-----------|-----------------|---------|
| At planting (basal) | DAP (Diammonium Phosphate, 18-46-0) | 1 bag (50 kg) | Phosphorus for root development |
| At planting (basal) | SOP (Sulphate of Potash, 0-0-50) or MOP (Muriate of Potash, 0-0-60) | 1 bag (50 kg) | Potassium for stalk strength and sugar transport |
| First top-dressing (45–60 days after planting) | Urea (46-0-0) | 1 bag (50 kg) | Nitrogen for tillering |
| Second top-dressing (90–100 days after planting) | Urea | 1–1.5 bags (50–75 kg) | Nitrogen for grand growth |
| Third top-dressing (120–140 days after planting) | Urea | 1 bag (50 kg) | Sustained nitrogen supply |

### Fertilizer Notes

- Apply DAP and potash at planting time in the furrow below the setts, not directly touching them, to avoid salt burn.
- Split urea applications into 3 doses. Broadcasting all nitrogen at once causes leaching losses and excessive vegetative growth at the expense of sugar.
- Apply urea when soil has adequate moisture. Never apply on dry soil followed by heavy irrigation — this causes rapid volatilization and runoff losses.
- Zinc deficiency is common in Pakistani sugarcane soils. If soil tests show low zinc (< 0.6 ppm), apply zinc sulphate (33%) at 5–8 kg per acre at planting.
- Sulphur deficiency is increasing. If symptoms appear (yellowing of young leaves, reduced sugar), apply gypsum or elemental sulphur based on soil test.

**Safety warning:** Store fertilizers in a dry, ventilated area away from children and livestock. Wear gloves and a mask when handling urea and DAP. Do not mix DAP with lime or wood ash.

## Diseases

### Red Rot

- **Cause:** Fungus *Glomerella tucumanensis* (also known as *Colletotrichum falcatum*).
- **Symptoms:** Reddish-brown discoloration inside the stalk when split open. Sour, alcoholic smell. Leaves wilt and yellow from the top down. White patches of fungal growth may appear on the rind. Affected stalks become hollow and lightweight.
- **When it occurs:** Most severe in waterlogged fields, during prolonged wet weather, and in ratoon crops. Can appear at any growth stage but is worst during grand growth and maturation.
- **What to do:**
  - Remove and burn infected clumps immediately — do not compost or leave in the field.
  - Use only disease-free, certified seed cane. Heat treatment of setts (52°C for 30 minutes) kills the fungus without harming buds.
  - Avoid ratooning severely infected fields. Plough out and replant with clean seed.
  - Improve drainage to prevent waterlogging.
  - Rotate with non-host crops (wheat, maize, legumes) for at least one season before replanting sugarcane.
- **Caution:** There is no effective fungicide cure once red rot is established in the field. Prevention through clean seed and good drainage is the only reliable strategy. Do not use setts from infected fields even if they look healthy externally.

### Smut

- **Cause:** Fungus *Sporisorium scitamineum*.
- **Symptoms:** Black, whip-like structures emerge from the top of infected stalks instead of a normal inflorescence. Infected plants are stunted with thin, grassy tillers. The black whip contains millions of spores that spread the disease.
- **When it occurs:** Symptoms typically appear 3–6 months after planting. Favoured by hot, dry conditions and stressed plants. Ratoon crops are more susceptible.
- **What to do:**
  - Rogue out infected plants as soon as whips appear. Bag the whip before pulling to prevent spore dispersal, then burn.
  - Plant resistant varieties (HSF-240 has moderate resistance).
  - Hot water treatment of seed cane (52°C for 30 minutes) effectively controls smut.
  - Avoid ratooning fields with more than 5% smut incidence.
- **Caution:** Smut spores survive in soil and on equipment. Clean tools and boots between fields. Do not take seed from fields where smut was observed even in previous seasons.

### Grassy Shoot Disease

- **Cause:** Phytoplasma (bacteria-like organism transmitted by leafhoppers).
- **Symptoms:** Excessive production of thin, grassy tillers with narrow, pale green to white leaves. No joint formation in affected shoots. Plants remain stunted and produce no millable cane.
- **When it occurs:** Can appear at any stage. Spread is linked to leafhopper populations, which peak in warm, humid weather. More common in ratoon crops and in fields adjacent to grass weeds.
- **What to do:**
  - Remove and destroy infected plants promptly.
  - Control leafhopper vectors with recommended insecticides during early crop stages.
  - Maintain weed-free fields, especially grass weeds that serve as alternate hosts.
  - Use disease-free seed from certified sources.
  - Roguing is most effective when done early (before 4 months). After that, infection has usually spread too far.
- **Caution:** Grassy shoot cannot be cured. Infected plants will never recover. Focus entirely on prevention through clean seed and vector control.

### Wilt

- **Cause:** Fungi *Fusarium* spp. and *Ceratocystis paradoxa*, often associated with nematode damage.
- **Symptoms:** Gradual wilting and yellowing of leaves starting from lower leaves. Stalks show internal browning or reddening (distinct from red rot's bright red color). Roots may be rotted. Plants pull up easily.
- **When it occurs:** Often associated with water stress, compacted soils, or nematode-infested fields. Can occur throughout the growing season.
- **What to do:**
  - Improve soil structure through deep ploughing and organic matter addition.
  - Manage nematodes through crop rotation (mustard, marigold as trap crops) or nematicide application based on soil test.
  - Ensure proper drainage and avoid compaction from heavy machinery on wet soil.
  - Use healthy, certified seed.
- **Caution:** Wilt is often a complex disease involving multiple pathogens. Accurate diagnosis requires lab testing. Submit samples to a plant pathology lab before investing in treatments.

## Pests

### Top Borer (*Scirpophaga excerptalis*)

- **Symptoms:** Dead hearts in young shoots (central leaf wilts and turns brown while outer leaves remain green). Bore holes near the top of the stalk with frass (insect droppings). Reduced tillering and stunted growth.
- **What to do:**
  - Collect and destroy egg masses on the underside of leaves during routine scouting (check weekly from 30 days after planting).
  - Release egg parasitoid *Trichogramma chilonis* cards at 40,000–50,000 per acre at 45-day intervals starting from 45 days after planting. Available from biocontrol laboratories.
  - If infestation exceeds 10% dead hearts, apply recommended insecticide (consult your local agriculture extension officer for current registered products and rates).
  - Clip and destroy affected tops before larvae move deeper into the stalk.
- **When:** Active from April through October. Peak damage occurs during tillering and early grand growth.
- **Caution:** Avoid broad-spectrum insecticides during flowering if intercropping with legumes. Follow label instructions precisely. Observe pre-harvest intervals.

### Stem Borer (*Chilo infuscatellus*)

- **Symptoms:** Dead hearts similar to top borer but bore holes are lower on the stalk. Larvae tunnel through internodes, weakening the stalk and reducing juice extraction. Frass visible at entry points.
- **What to do:**
  - Same biological control approach as top borer using *Trichogramma* releases.
  - Maintain field hygiene: remove stubble and trash after harvest to eliminate overwintering larvae.
  - Deep ploughing after harvest exposes pupae to predators and sun.
  - Insecticide application only when economic threshold is exceeded (consult extension service).
- **When:** Most damaging during tillering to early elongation (months 2–5). Second-generation damage can occur later.
- **Caution:** Stem borer damage is often confused with top borer. Correct identification matters for timing control measures. Cut open affected stalks to identify the species.

### Pyrilla (*Pyrilla perpusilla*) — Major Pest

- **Symptoms:** Yellowish-green planthoppers suck sap from the underside of leaves, causing yellowing, drying, and sooty mould growth on honeydew excretions. Heavy infestations cause significant yield and sugar loss. Leaves develop brown, dried patches. Severe attacks can defoliate entire fields.
- **What to do:**
  - Monitor weekly from July onward. Check undersides of lower leaves for nymphs and adults.
  - Economic threshold: 5–10 nymphs/adults per leaf on average across 20 randomly sampled plants.
  - Biological control: conserve natural enemies (spiders, ladybird beetles, lacewings). Avoid unnecessary insecticide applications that kill beneficials.
  - When threshold is exceeded, apply recommended insecticide targeting nymphs (nymphs are easier to control than adults). Consult extension officer for current product recommendations.
  - Remove and destroy heavily infested lower leaves to reduce population and improve spray penetration.
  - Neem-based formulations can suppress light infestations with less harm to beneficial insects.
- **When:** Pyrilla populations build up from July–August and peak during September–October. Monsoon humidity favours outbreaks.
- **Caution:** Pyrilla outbreaks can develop rapidly. Weekly monitoring during the buildup period is essential — do not wait for visible damage. Over-reliance on chemical control leads to resistance and destroys natural enemy populations that provide free suppression. Alternate insecticide classes if repeated applications are necessary.

### White Grub (*Holotrichia consanguinea* and related species)

- **Symptoms:** Patches of wilting and dying plants that can be pulled up easily. C-shaped, creamy-white larvae feeding on roots below the soil surface. Damage appears as irregular patches rather than individual scattered plants.
- **What to do:**
  - Scout for grubs during land preparation. Dig 30 cm x 30 cm x 30 cm pits at random locations and count grubs. More than 2–3 grubs per pit warrants treatment.
  - During land preparation, collect and destroy exposed grubs manually. Birds following the plough are natural allies.
  - Apply recommended soil-applied insecticide at planting if grub counts exceed threshold.
  - Maintain field boundaries and hedgerows to support bird populations that feed on adult beetles.
  - Light traps during evening hours in June–July can capture adult beetles before egg-laying.
- **When:** Larval damage peaks during July–September when grubs are actively feeding near the surface. Adults emerge with monsoon rains.
- **Caution:** White grub damage is often discovered too late. By the time above-ground wilting is visible, root damage is already severe. Preventive management at planting is far more effective than curative treatment. Soil insecticides must be applied at planting time — post-emergence applications rarely reach the feeding zone effectively.

## Common Problems

### Poor Germination

- **Causes:** Old or diseased seed, planting too deep (> 10 cm), dry soil at planting, waterlogging, soil crusting, pest damage (white grub, termites).
- **Solutions:**
  - Use fresh, certified seed with at least 3 viable buds per sett.
  - Plant at 8–10 cm depth in moist soil. Cover lightly in heavy soils.
  - Irrigate immediately after planting (light irrigation).
  - Break soil crust with light harrowing if crusting occurs before emergence.
  - Treat setts with recommended fungicide/insecticide seed treatment before planting.
  - Target 80–90% germination. Below 70%, gap-filling with pre-sprouted setts is necessary within 30–45 days of planting.

### Lodging (Falling Over)

- **Causes:** Excessive nitrogen, tall/thin varieties, shallow rooting, wind storms, waterlogged soil weakening root anchorage, high planting density.
- **Solutions:**
  - Balance nitrogen with adequate potassium (potassium strengthens stalks).
  - Choose lodging-resistant varieties (thick-stalked types like CPF-246).
  - Earth up (mound soil around the base) at 90–100 days after planting to improve anchorage.
  - Avoid excessive irrigation in windy periods.
  - Maintain recommended spacing (row-to-row 75–90 cm).
- **Impact:** Lodged cane loses 15–30% yield due to reduced photosynthesis, harvesting difficulty, and increased pest/disease entry through damaged stalks.

### Low Sugar Recovery

- **Causes:** Harvesting immature cane, delayed processing after cutting (> 48 hours), varietal characteristics, excessive nitrogen near harvest, high temperatures during storage/transport.
- **Solutions:**
  - Harvest only mature cane. Check Brix with a hand refractometer — target > 18° Brix.
  - Coordinate harvest with mill schedule to minimize field-to-crusher time. Cut cane should reach the mill within 24–48 hours.
  - Reduce nitrogen application after month 8. Last urea dose should be no later than 120 days before expected harvest.
  - Transport cane in covered trucks or during cooler hours to reduce sucrose inversion.
  - Select high-sugar varieties appropriate for your region.

## Harvesting and Ratoon Management

### Maturity Indicators

- **Brix reading:** Hand refractometer shows > 18° Brix in middle internodes.
- **Leaf sheath drying:** Lower leaf sheaths begin to loosen and dry naturally.
- **Stalk hardness:** Mature stalks resist thumbnail pressure.
- **Age:** Generally 10–12 months for plant cane, 9–11 months for ratoon.
- **Mill announcement:** Sugar mills announce opening dates based on regional maturity surveys.

### Harvest Method

- Cut stalks at ground level with a sharp knife or machete. A clean cut promotes better ratoon regrowth.
- Strip leaves and tops in the field. Tops contain low sugar and high impurities — mills penalize excess trash.
- Bundle and transport promptly.

### Ratoon Management

Ratooning (regrowth from harvested stubble) saves the cost of replanting but yields decline with each successive ratoon. Most farmers in Pakistan take 1–2 ratoons before replanting.

- **After harvest:** Trim stubble to 5–8 cm above ground. Remove trash and earth up around stubble.
- **Irrigate within 7–10 days** of harvest to stimulate sprouting.
- **Apply basal fertilizer** (DAP + potash) along the row and cover with soil. Ratoon crops need the same phosphorus and potassium as plant cane.
- **Gap-fill** missing stools within 30 days using pre-sprouted setts from the same field.
- **Monitor closely** for smut and grassy shoot — ratoon crops are more susceptible. Rogue aggressively.
- **Yield expectation:** First ratoon typically yields 70–85% of plant cane. Second ratoon yields 50–65%. Beyond two ratoons, replanting is usually more profitable.

### Yield Benchmarks

- **Average yield in Pakistan:** 25–30 tonnes per acre (62–74 tonnes per hectare).
- **Good commercial yield:** 35–45 tonnes per acre.
- **Progressive farmer benchmark:** 50+ tonnes per acre with certified seed, balanced fertilization, and timely irrigation.
- **Sugar recovery:** National average is approximately 9.5–10%. Progressive farms achieve 10.5–11%.
