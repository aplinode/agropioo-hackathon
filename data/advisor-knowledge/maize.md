# Maize (Corn) Farming Guide — Pakistan

## Overview

Maize is the third most important cereal crop in Pakistan after wheat and rice. It is grown as both a Kharif (monsoon) crop and a spring crop. Maize serves as food grain, poultry and livestock feed, and industrial raw material (starch, oil, cornflakes).

Major growing areas include **Punjab** (Sahiwal, Okara, Pakpattan, Vehari, Khanewal, Toba Tek Singh districts), **Khyber Pakhtunkhwa** (Peshawar, Mardan, Swabi, Abbottabad, Mansehra), and parts of **Sindh** and **Azad Kashmir**. KPK accounts for roughly half of national maize area, while Punjab contributes the majority of hybrid maize production.

### Hybrid vs Open-Pollinated Varieties

**Hybrid varieties** yield 30–50% more than open-pollinated varieties but require purchasing new seed each season. They respond better to fertilizer and irrigation.

| Variety | Type | Key Traits | Region |
|---------|------|-----------|--------|
| **Pioneer 30Y87** | Hybrid | High yield potential (40–50 maunds/acre), good standability, medium maturity (95–100 days) | Punjab, KPK |
| **DKC-6525** (DeKalb) | Hybrid | Excellent yield, strong stalk, good disease tolerance, medium-late maturity | Punjab |
| **Azam** | Open-pollinated | Reliable performer, moderate yield (25–30 maunds/acre), adaptable, seed can be saved | KPK, rainfed areas |
| **Sarhad White** | Open-pollinated | White grain preferred for human consumption, adapted to KPK conditions | KPK |
| **MMRI Yellow** | Open-pollinated | Developed by Maize & Millet Research Institute Sahiwal, good for Punjab conditions | Punjab |
| **Pioneer 30V87** | Hybrid | Early maturity (85–90 days), suitable for late sowing or relay cropping | Punjab |

**Seed selection guidance:** If you have reliable irrigation and can invest in inputs, choose hybrids. For rainfed or marginal lands, or if saving seed is important, open-pollinated varieties like Azam are practical. Always buy certified seed from authorized dealers. Counterfeit seed is a widespread problem — verify packaging and purchase from reputable agro-shops.

## Crop Calendar

### Sowing Windows

| Season | Sowing Period | Harvest Period | Notes |
|--------|--------------|----------------|-------|
| Spring | February 15 – March 15 | June–July | Grown in Punjab and KPK. Avoid sowing before Feb 15 — cold soil reduces germination. |
| Kharif (Monsoon) | July 1 – August 15 | October–November | Main maize season. Aligns with monsoon rains. Late sowing (after Aug 15) significantly reduces yield. |

**Optimal sowing window:** The first two weeks of the respective season give the best yields. Each week of delay beyond the optimal window can reduce yield by 5–10%.

### Growth Stages

1. **Emergence (Days 5–10):** Seedlings break through soil. Uniform emergence depends on proper planting depth (3–5 cm) and soil moisture.
2. **Vegetative growth V1–V6 (Days 10–40):** Leaf stages. Root system establishes. Weed competition is most damaging during this phase.
3. **Tasseling / VT (Days 45–55):** Male flowers (tassel) emerge at the top. Pollen shed begins. **Most critical stage for water stress.**
4. **Silking / R1 (Days 50–60):** Silk threads emerge from ear shoots. Pollination occurs. **Second most critical stage.** Water or heat stress during silking causes barren cobs or poor kernel set.
5. **Grain filling R2–R5 (Days 55–85):** Kernels develop and fill. Moisture and nutrient demand remains high. Stress during this period reduces kernel weight.
6. **Physiological maturity R6 (Days 85–110):** Black layer forms at kernel base. Grain moisture drops to 30–35%. Crop is physiologically mature.
7. **Harvest maturity:** Grain moisture reaches 20–25% for mechanical harvest, or 14–16% for safe storage.

Total duration varies by variety: early hybrids mature in 85–90 days, medium in 95–100 days, and late/open-pollinated in 100–120 days.

## Irrigation Schedule

Maize requires **450–600 mm** of water over its growing season. While less water-intensive than sugarcane or rice, maize is highly sensitive to water stress at specific growth stages.

### Critical Irrigation Stages

| Stage | Timing | Why It Matters |
|-------|--------|---------------|
| Pre-sowing | Before planting | Ensures uniform germination. Sow in adequately moist soil. |
| Knee-high stage (V6–V8) | ~30–35 days after sowing | Supports rapid vegetative growth and tassel initiation. |
| **Tasseling** | ~45–55 days after sowing | **MOST CRITICAL.** Even 2–3 days of water stress during tasseling can reduce yield by 20–30%. Pollen viability drops sharply under drought. |
| **Silking** | ~50–60 days after sowing | **SECOND MOST CRITICAL.** Silks desiccate quickly without adequate moisture. Failed pollination = barren cobs. |
| Grain filling | Days 55–85 | Sustains kernel weight. Stress shrinks kernels and reduces test weight. |
| Dough stage | ~75–85 days | Final grain weight determination. Last important irrigation. |

### Practical Schedule

- **Spring maize:** 4–6 irrigations depending on rainfall and soil type. Interval: 10–15 days during vegetative stage, 7–10 days during tasseling to grain filling.
- **Kharif maize:** 2–4 supplemental irrigations depending on monsoon distribution. Monsoon rains may cover some needs, but gaps in rainfall during tasseling/silking must be filled with irrigation.
- Sandy soils require more frequent, lighter irrigation. Clay soils hold moisture longer.

**Safety warning:** Never irrigate during midday heat (11 AM–3 PM) in summer. Water evaporates rapidly and workers face heat stress. Irrigate in early morning or evening.

## Fertilizer Management

Maize is a **heavy nitrogen feeder**. It responds strongly to balanced fertilization, especially hybrids. Nutrient removal per acre for a 35-maund crop is approximately 70 kg N, 25 kg P₂O₅, and 30 kg K₂O.

### Fertilizer Schedule (per acre)

| Timing | Fertilizer | Rate (per acre) | Purpose |
|--------|-----------|-----------------|---------|
| At sowing (basal) | DAP (18-46-0) | 1 bag (50 kg) | Phosphorus for root development and early vigour |
| At sowing (basal) | SOP (0-0-50) or MOP (0-0-60) | ½–1 bag (25–50 kg) | Potassium for stalk strength and water regulation |
| First top-dressing (knee-high, ~30 days) | Urea (46-0-0) | 1 bag (50 kg) | Nitrogen for rapid vegetative growth |
| Second top-dressing (pre-tasseling, ~45 days) | Urea | 1 bag (50 kg) | Nitrogen for tasseling, silking, and grain set |

**Total per acre:** 2 bags Urea + 1 bag DAP + ½–1 bag potash. Hybrids at high yield targets may benefit from an additional ½ bag Urea at silking.

### Fertilizer Notes

- Place DAP and potash in bands beside and below the seed row at planting, not mixed directly with seed (salt injury risk).
- Split nitrogen into at least 2 applications. Single-application nitrogen is wasted through leaching in sandy soils and denitrification in waterlogged soils.
- Apply urea on moist soil and incorporate lightly or irrigate within 24 hours to minimize ammonia volatilization.
- Zinc deficiency is very common in Pakistani maize-growing soils. Apply zinc sulphate (33%) at 5–8 kg per acre at sowing, or use zinc-enriched DAP if available. Zinc-deficient maize shows white/yellow stripes between veins on younger leaves and stunted growth.
- Soil testing is strongly recommended before deciding fertilizer rates. Over-fertilizing wastes money and can cause lodging.

**Safety warning:** Wear gloves and a dust mask when handling fertilizers. Store in a locked, dry location away from children, animals, and food. Do not store urea and DAP together with pesticides.

## Diseases

### Downy Mildew

- **Cause:** Fungi *Peronosclerospora sorghi* and related species.
- **Symptoms:** Pale green to yellowish streaks on leaves running parallel to veins. White, downy fungal growth on the underside of leaves, especially in cool, humid mornings. Severely infected plants are stunted and may fail to produce cobs. Systemic infection causes complete chlorosis and plant death.
- **What to do:**
  - Use resistant varieties. Several hybrids have good downy mildew resistance.
  - Treat seed with metalaxyl-based fungicide before sowing (available as commercial seed treatment).
  - Avoid sowing in poorly drained fields or during extended cool, wet periods.
  - Rogue out infected plants early to reduce inoculum spread.
  - Crop rotation with non-cereal crops (legumes, cotton) breaks the disease cycle.
- **When:** Favored by cool temperatures (15–25°C) and high humidity. Most common in spring maize and early Kharif plantings in humid areas.
- **Caution:** Downy mildew spreads rapidly under favorable conditions. Once systemic infection is established, no fungicide will cure it. Prevention through resistant varieties and seed treatment is essential.

### Leaf Blight (Northern Corn Leaf Blight)

- **Cause:** Fungus *Exserohilum turcicum*.
- **Symptoms:** Long, elliptical, grey-green lesions (5–15 cm) on leaves, starting from lower leaves and progressing upward. Lesions turn tan/brown with age. Under humid conditions, dark olive-green spore masses form on lesion surfaces. Severe infections cause premature leaf death and yield loss.
- **What to do:**
  - Plant resistant hybrids. Many commercial hybrids carry partial resistance genes.
  - Apply foliar fungicide (e.g., azoxystrobin or propiconazole-based products) if disease appears before tasseling and weather forecasts predict continued humidity. Application after grain fill has started is rarely economical.
  - Destroy crop residues after harvest to reduce carryover inoculum.
  - Rotate with non-host crops.
- **When:** Develops during warm (18–27°C), humid weather. Most damaging when lesions reach the ear leaf before or during grain filling.
- **Caution:** Do not confuse leaf blight with herbicide injury or nutrient deficiency. Leaf blight lesions have distinct elliptical shapes with defined margins. If uncertain, submit a sample to a plant pathology lab.

### Stalk Rot

- **Cause:** Multiple fungi including *Fusarium*, *Gibberella*, *Macrophomina*, and *Pythium*. Often a complex of organisms exploiting stressed plants.
- **Symptoms:** Lower internodes become soft, discolored (brown to pink/reddish inside), and collapse. Plants lodge or break at the base. Premature plant death with shriveled, lightweight cobs. Pink or white fungal growth may be visible inside split stalks.
- **What to do:**
  - Maintain balanced fertility. Potassium deficiency and excessive nitrogen predispose plants to stalk rot.
  - Avoid water stress during grain filling. Stressed plants redirect sugars from stalks to grain, weakening stalk tissue.
  - Choose hybrids with good stalk strength ratings.
  - Harvest promptly at physiological maturity. Delayed harvest increases lodging losses.
  - Ensure adequate plant population — overcrowding stresses plants and promotes stalk rot.
- **When:** Symptoms typically appear during grain filling to maturity (R4–R6). Often noticed first as scattered lodged plants.
- **Caution:** Stalk rot is largely a management-preventable disease. Once stalks are compromised, no treatment exists. Focus on balanced nutrition, adequate water, and appropriate plant density. Push-test stalks at grain filling: push plants 15 cm from vertical — if more than 10–15% fail to snap back, prioritize early harvest.

### Rust (Common Rust and Southern Rust)

- **Cause:** Fungi *Puccinia sorghi* (common rust) and *Puccinia polysora* (southern rust).
- **Symptoms:** Small, circular to elongate, cinnamon-brown pustules scattered on both leaf surfaces. Common rust pustules are darker and more scattered; southern rust pustules are lighter orange and concentrated on upper leaf surfaces. Heavy infections cause leaf senescence.
- **What to do:**
  - Most hybrids have adequate resistance to common rust. Southern rust is more aggressive and may require fungicide.
  - Apply foliar fungicide if southern rust appears before or during grain filling and conditions remain humid.
  - Scout regularly from tasseling onward. Early detection allows timely fungicide application.
  - Remove volunteer maize plants between seasons that serve as green bridges for the pathogen.
- **When:** Common rust favors cool (16–23°C) conditions; southern rust favors warm (24–30°C), humid conditions. Southern rust typically arrives later in the season via windborne spores.
- **Caution:** Southern rust can spread explosively under favorable conditions. If identified in your district, alert neighboring farmers. Do not rely solely on varietal resistance for southern rust — it can overcome many resistance genes.

## Pests

### Fall Armyworm (*Spodoptera frugiperda*) — MAJOR Recent Pest

Fall armyworm (FAW) arrived in Pakistan around 2019 and has since become the single most destructive maize pest across the country. It can cause 30–70% yield loss if unmanaged.

- **Symptoms:**
  - Young larvae scrape leaf surfaces creating "windowpane" damage.
  - Older larvae bore into the whorl and feed on emerging leaves, creating characteristic ragged holes and sawdust-like frass in the funnel.
  - Larvae also bore into developing ears through the silk channel, damaging kernels and inviting secondary fungal infections.
  - Damage is concentrated in the whorl and ear zone. Presence of moist, sawdust-like frass in the whorl is a diagnostic sign.
- **What to do:**
  - **Scout weekly** from emergence through grain filling. Part the whorl and inspect for larvae and frass.
  - **Hand-picking:** In small plots, collect and destroy larvae from whorls during early morning or evening. Drop into soapy water.
  - **Biological control:** Apply *Bacillus thuringiensis* (Bt) spray or nucleopolyhedrovirus (NPV) formulations targeting young larvae (1st–3rd instar). These are selective and preserve beneficial insects. Available from agricultural input suppliers.
  - **Chemical control:** When infestation exceeds 20% of plants showing fresh whorl damage, apply recommended insecticide. Products containing emamectin benzoate, chlorantraniliprole, or spinetoram have shown effectiveness against FAW in Pakistan. **Rotate insecticide classes** to prevent resistance.
  - **Timing is everything:** Insecticides are effective ONLY against young larvae (small, < 1 cm) before they burrow deep into the whorl or ear. Large larvae inside the whorl are nearly impossible to kill with sprays.
  - **Trap crops:** Plant Napier grass or other attractive grasses around field borders to attract egg-laying moths away from maize.
  - **Push-pull strategy:** Intercrop with Desmodium (repels FAW) and plant Napier grass as a border trap crop. This integrated approach has proven effective in South Asian conditions.
- **When:** FAW is active year-round in southern Pakistan and during the warm months (April–November) in Punjab and KPK. Multiple overlapping generations make continuous monitoring essential.
- **Caution:** FAW has developed resistance to several insecticide classes worldwide. Never use the same insecticide class repeatedly. Alternate between at least 3 different modes of action. Do NOT use banned or restricted pesticides (e.g., monocrotophos, carbofuran). Follow label directions strictly. Wear protective equipment (gloves, mask, long sleeves) when spraying. Report suspected pesticide poisoning to medical services immediately.

### Shoot Fly (*Atherigona soccata*)

- **Symptoms:** Maggots feed inside the central shoot, causing dead heart (central leaf dies while outer leaves remain green). Similar to stem borer dead heart but occurs in younger plants (2–4 leaf stage). Affected plants may produce excessive side tillers.
- **What to do:**
  - Sow early in the recommended window. Early-sown crops escape peak shoot fly activity.
  - Increase seed rate by 10–15% and rogue out infested plants during thinning.
  - Seed treatment with imidacloprid or thiamethoxam provides protection during the vulnerable seedling stage.
  - Maintain field hygiene: remove crop residues and volunteer plants that harbor the pest.
- **When:** Most damaging to spring maize sown in February–March. Shoot fly populations peak in March–April. Kharif maize usually escapes severe damage.
- **Caution:** Shoot fly damage window is narrow (first 3–4 weeks after emergence). After the 6-leaf stage, plants outgrow susceptibility. Protect the seedling stage specifically.

### Stem Borer (*Chilo partellus*)

- **Symptoms:** Dead hearts in young plants. Bore holes with frass on stalks. Larvae tunnel through stalks weakening them and disrupting nutrient flow. Damaged stalks break easily and produce poorly filled cobs.
- **What to do:**
  - Release *Trichogramma chilonis* egg parasitoid cards at 30,000–40,000 per acre during peak egg-laying periods.
  - Apply Bt spray targeting young larvae before they enter stalks.
  - Deep ploughing after harvest destroys overwintering larvae in stubble.
  - Chemical control if infestation exceeds 10% dead hearts and biological control is insufficient.
- **When:** Active throughout the growing season. Two to three generations per crop. First generation damages young plants; later generations affect stalk integrity.
- **Caution:** Stem borer larvae are protected inside stalks once they enter. Surface-applied insecticides are ineffective against tunneled larvae. Timing interventions to target young larvae or eggs is critical.

## Common Problems

### Poor Cob Filling (Tip Dieback / Kernel Abortion)

- **Causes:** Water stress during silking and grain filling, excessive plant population, nutrient deficiency (especially nitrogen and zinc), heat stress (> 35°C) during pollination, poor pollen viability.
- **Solutions:**
  - Ensure adequate irrigation during tasseling and silking. This is non-negotiable.
  - Follow recommended plant population: 22,000–27,000 plants per acre for hybrids, 18,000–22,000 for open-pollinated varieties. Overcrowding is a leading cause of tip dieback.
  - Ensure adequate nitrogen at pre-tasseling stage.
  - Apply zinc at sowing to prevent zinc-related kernel abortion.
  - Choose heat-tolerant varieties for areas prone to high temperatures during reproductive stages.

### Lodging

- **Causes:** Stalk rot diseases, excessive nitrogen with insufficient potassium, high winds, waterlogged soil, excessive plant population, hybrid susceptibility.
- **Solutions:**
  - Maintain balanced NPK nutrition. Adequate potassium strengthens stalks.
  - Choose hybrids with strong stalk ratings.
  - Avoid excessive nitrogen. More is not always better.
  - Ensure proper drainage.
  - Harvest promptly at physiological maturity. Every day of delay increases lodging risk.
  - Push-test stalks at dough stage to assess lodging risk and plan early harvest if needed.

## Harvesting and Drying

### When to Harvest

- **Physiological maturity:** Black layer visible at kernel base. Grain moisture ~30–35%. This is the earliest safe harvest point.
- **For manual harvest:** Wait until husks are dry and papery, grain moisture ~20–25%. Usually 2–3 weeks after black layer.
- **For mechanical harvest:** Target grain moisture 20–25% to minimize shattering and cracking.
- **Do not delay harvest unnecessarily.** Field losses from birds, rodents, stalk rot, and weather increase every day past maturity.

### Harvest Method

- Break cobs from stalks manually or use combine harvester.
- Remove husks promptly to speed drying and reduce mold risk.
- Sort out damaged, diseased, or insect-infested cobs before storage.

### Drying

Safe storage moisture for maize grain is **12–14%**. Grain stored above 14% moisture develops aflatoxin-producing molds (*Aspergillus flavus*), which render grain unsafe for human and animal consumption.

- **Sun drying:** Spread grain in thin layers (5–7 cm) on clean, hard surfaces (concrete, tarpaulins — not bare soil). Turn frequently. Takes 3–5 days depending on weather. Cover at night and during rain.
- **Mechanical drying:** Batch or continuous-flow dryers reduce moisture reliably regardless of weather. Preferred for large quantities and when monsoon humidity prevents sun drying.
- **Moisture testing:** Use a grain moisture meter before storage. Do not guess. Alternatively, bite-test: a properly dried kernel resists biting and cracks cleanly; a moist kernel dents.
- **Storage:** Store in clean, dry, well-ventilated bins or hermetic bags (e.g., PICS bags, GrainPro). Keep off the floor on pallets. Inspect monthly for heating, mold, or insect activity. Fumigate if insect infestation is detected.

**Safety warning:** Aflatoxin-contaminated maize is a serious health hazard causing liver cancer and acute toxicity. NEVER feed moldy or suspect grain to livestock or humans. If grain smells musty, shows visible mold, or tests positive for aflatoxin above regulatory limits, dispose of it safely. Do not blend contaminated grain with clean grain.

### Post-Harvest Stalk Management

- Chop and incorporate stalks into soil to improve organic matter, OR
- Remove and use as fodder (maize stover is valuable livestock feed), OR
- Leave standing briefly as windbreak for subsequent crops, then remove.
- Never leave stalks standing indefinitely — they harbor stem borer and stalk rot pathogens for the next season.
