# Rice — Complete Crop Guide for Pakistan

## Overview
Rice is Pakistan's second most important cereal crop and the country's top agricultural export commodity. It is a Kharif (summer) crop grown on approximately 2.5–3 million hectares, primarily in Punjab (Gujranwala, Sialkot, Sheikhupura, Hafizabad, Mandi Bahauddin, Narowal) and Sindh (Larkana, Sukkur, Jacobabad, Shikarpur, Dadu). Smaller areas are cultivated in KPK (Swat, Buner, Malakand). Pakistan grows two broad categories of rice:

- **Basmati varieties:** Aromatic, long-grain rice with premium export value. Key varieties include Super Basmati, PK-1121 (extra-long grain), KS-282, and newer releases such as PK-2021 from RRI Kala Shah Kaku. Basmati rice has a longer growing season (120–145 days to maturity) and is sensitive to high temperatures during grain filling.
- **Coarse (non-Basmati) varieties:** Shorter duration (90–110 days), higher-yielding, used for domestic consumption and parboiled rice exports. Key varieties include IRRI-6, IRRI-9, and various local selections. These tolerate wider temperature ranges and are preferred in Sindh's hotter climate.

Farmers should always source certified seed from approved dealers. Counterfeit or uncertified seed is a recurring problem that leads to poor germination, disease carryover, and varietal mixing that destroys Basmati purity premiums.

## Nursery Management

### Seed Rate
- **Basmati varieties:** 8–10 kg per acre (nursery seed requirement)
- **Coarse varieties:** 10–12 kg per acre
- Use only certified, disease-free seed

### Seed Treatment
1. Soak seed in clean water for 24 hours
2. Pre-sprout by keeping soaked seed in gunny sacks for 24–48 hours until tiny white tips appear
3. Treat pre-sprouted seed with fungicide: carbendazim (Bavistin) at 2 g per kg seed, or thiram at 3 g per kg seed, to prevent seedling blast and damping-off

### Nursery Bed Preparation
1. Select a well-drained, raised site near a reliable water source — avoid low-lying spots where cold water collects
2. Prepare fine, puddled soil beds 1.5 m wide with drainage channels between beds
3. Incorporate well-decomposed farmyard manure (FYM) at 2–3 kg per square meter of nursery bed
4. Level beds carefully — uneven beds cause patchy germination
5. Broadcast pre-sprouted seed evenly on wet mud; press gently into soil surface
6. Maintain moist conditions (not flooded) for the first 7–10 days; then keep 1–2 cm standing water

### Transplanting Age and Method
- **Optimal seedling age:** 25–30 days for Basmati; 20–25 days for coarse varieties
- Older seedlings (>35 days) suffer transplant shock, delayed tillering, and reduced yield
- Transplant 2–3 seedlings per hill at spacing of 20 cm x 20 cm (Basmati) or 15 cm x 15 cm (coarse)
- Transplant in the late afternoon or on cloudy days to reduce wilting
- Maintain 2–3 cm standing water in the main field at transplanting

## Irrigation Schedule

### Continuous Flooding (Traditional Method)
- Maintain 3–5 cm (1.5–2 inches) of standing water throughout the vegetative and reproductive stages
- This suppresses weeds and stabilizes soil temperature
- Drain the field completely 10–15 days before harvest to allow soil to firm up for harvesting equipment or manual cutting

### Alternate Wetting and Drying (AWD — Water-Saving Method)
AWD can save 15–30% irrigation water without reducing yield when practiced correctly:
1. Flood the field to 5 cm depth
2. Allow water to recede naturally until the soil surface is just visible (no standing water, but soil remains saturated)
3. Re-flood to 5 cm
4. Repeat this cycle throughout the growing season
5. **Critical exception:** Do NOT practice AWD during panicle initiation through flowering (approximately 45–85 days after transplanting). Keep continuous flooding during this period — water stress at flowering causes empty grains and irreversible yield loss

**When to avoid AWD:** On sandy soils that drain too fast, in fields with severe weed pressure, and during extended dry spells when re-flooding water may not be available.

**Laser land leveling:** Strongly recommended before transplanting. Level fields hold water uniformly, reduce total water use, and improve fertilizer efficiency. Uneven fields waste water in low spots and starve plants on high spots.

## Fertilizer Schedule

### Recommended Nutrient Doses Per Acre

| Nutrient | Total Dose Per Acre | Basal (before/at transplanting) | Top Dress |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nitrogen (N) | 90 kg (approx. 2 bags urea) | One-third at transplanting | One-third at tillering (25–30 DAT); one-third at panicle initiation (45–50 DAT) |
| Phosphorus (P₂O₅) | 60 kg (approx. 1 bag DAP) | Full dose before transplanting | — |
| Potash (K₂O) | 40 kg SOP | Half before transplanting | Half at panicle initiation |
| Zinc | 10–12 kg zinc sulphate | Full dose before transplanting | — |

**Recommended fertilizers per acre (practical guide):**
- **DAP (Diammonium Phosphate):** 1 bag (50 kg) — incorporate into puddled soil before transplanting
- **Urea:** 2 bags (100 kg total) — split into three equal applications: at transplanting, at active tillering (25–30 days after transplanting), and at panicle initiation (45–50 days after transplanting)
- **SOP (Sulphate of Potash):** 40 kg — half basal, half at panicle initiation. Prefer SOP over MOP (Muriate of Potash) in saline/sodic soils common in Punjab
- **Zinc Sulphate (33% Zn):** 10–12 kg per acre — apply to puddled soil before transplanting. If zinc deficiency symptoms appear mid-season, foliar spray zinc sulphate at 0.5% solution (5 g per litre water)

**Important notes:**
- Always base fertilizer rates on a soil test when possible. The above doses are general recommendations for medium-fertility soils.
- Do not apply all nitrogen at once — excessive early nitrogen causes lodging, disease susceptibility, and pest attraction.
- Phosphorus must be applied basally; it does not move easily in flooded soil.
- Zinc deficiency is extremely widespread in Pakistani rice soils. Do not skip zinc application even if other nutrients seem adequate.

## Diseases

### Blast (Leaf Blast and Neck Blast)
**Symptoms:**
- **Leaf blast:** Diamond-shaped or spindle-shaped grey-green lesions with brown borders appear on leaves. Lesions enlarge and coalesce, causing entire leaves to wither and die. Most severe in the nursery and early vegetative stage.
- **Neck blast:** Dark brown to black necrotic lesions on the panicle neck (node just below the grain head). Infected panicles turn white, fail to fill grain, and may break at the neck. Neck blast is far more damaging than leaf blast because it directly destroys yield.

**Cause:** Fungus *Magnaporthe oryzae* (syn. *Pyricularia oryzae*). Favored by cool nights (20–25°C), high humidity, frequent rain or dew, and excessive nitrogen. Leaf blast favors younger tissue; neck blast attacks at heading.

**What to do:**
1. Use resistant or tolerant varieties — ask your seed dealer for blast-resistant options suited to your area
2. Avoid excessive nitrogen, especially single large doses — split applications reduce blast severity
3. Scout fields weekly from transplanting through heading; check lower canopy and shaded areas first
4. At first sign of leaf blast, spray fungicide immediately: tricyclazole (Beam 75WP) at 100 g per acre, or isoprothiolane (Fuji-One 40EC) at 300 ml per acre
5. For neck blast prevention, spray at boot-to-heading stage (just before panicles emerge): tricyclazole at 100 g per acre plus mancozeb (Dithane M-45) at 400 g per acre as a tank mix
6. Repeat spray after 10–14 days if weather remains favorable for disease (rain, high humidity)
7. Remove and destroy heavily infected plant debris after harvest to reduce inoculum for next season

**When:** Leaf blast: nursery stage through tillering (June–August). Neck blast: booting through heading (August–September in Basmati zone). Highest risk during monsoon rains with cool nights.

**Caution:** Do not spray fungicides during full bloom (midday flowering). Apply in late afternoon or early morning. Wear gloves, mask, and protective clothing. Observe pre-harvest interval (PHI) stated on the product label — typically 14–21 days for tricyclazole.

### Bacterial Leaf Blight (BLB)
**Symptoms:**
- Water-soaked, yellowish-green streaks appear on leaf margins, usually starting at the leaf tip
- Lesions rapidly expand along the leaf margin, turning yellow-white then grey-white as tissue dies
- Lesions have wavy, irregular margins (unlike fungal diseases which tend to have defined borders)
- In severe cases, entire leaves bleach white and die; bacterial ooze (milky droplets) may be visible on fresh lesions in the morning
- Can also cause "kresek" (seedling wilt) in nurseries — sudden wilting and death of young seedlings

**Cause:** Bacterium *Xanthomonas oryzae* pv. *oryzae*. Enters through wounds caused by wind, rain, insects, or mechanical injury. Spreads rapidly in warm (25–30°C), humid conditions with standing water. Excessive nitrogen and flood irrigation accelerate spread.

**What to do:**
1. Use BLB-resistant varieties — this is the most effective control; consult local extension for current resistant varieties
2. Reduce nitrogen rates in BLB-prone fields and areas with known history
3. Avoid clipping seedling tips during transplanting (creates entry wounds)
4. At first symptom appearance, spray bactericide: streptocycline (Strepto 100) at 15 g per 100 litres water, or copper oxychloride (Blitox 50WP) at 300 g per acre
5. Repeat spray every 7–10 days for 2–3 applications if disease is spreading
6. Improve field drainage — stagnant water spreads bacteria between plants
7. Rogue out severely infected plants and bury or burn them away from the field

**When:** Most severe during July–September in Punjab and June–August in Sindh. Kresek phase occurs in nurseries within 1–2 weeks of transplanting. Peak risk follows heavy monsoon rains combined with high temperatures.

**Caution:** Bactericides are protectants, not cures — they slow spread but cannot reverse existing damage. Early detection and immediate action are critical. Copper-based products can cause phytotoxicity in hot weather (>35°C); apply in cooler evening hours. Streptocycline is an antibiotic — handle with care and follow label instructions precisely.

### Brown Spot
**Symptoms:**
- Small, oval to circular, dark brown spots (1–3 mm) scattered across leaf surfaces
- Spots have grey centres with dark brown borders — resemble cigarette burns
- Heavily infected leaves turn yellow-brown and die prematurely
- Seeds may also show brown discoloration, reducing milling quality and market value
- Unlike blast, spots are randomly distributed (not diamond-shaped) and do not coalesce into large lesions

**Cause:** Fungus *Bipolaris oryzae* (syn. *Helminthosporium oryzae*). Seed-borne and residue-borne. Favored by warm temperatures (25–30°C), high humidity, and nutrient-deficient soils (especially potassium and silicon deficiency). Common in poorly drained fields and late-planted crops.

**What to do:**
1. Treat seed with fungicide before sowing: carbendazim (Bavistin) at 2 g per kg seed, or thiram at 3 g per kg seed
2. Ensure balanced nutrition — adequate potassium and phosphorus reduce severity
3. Spray fungicide at first symptom appearance: carbendazim (Bavistin 50WP) at 200 g per acre, or mancozeb (Dithane M-45) at 400 g per acre
4. Repeat spray after 10–14 days if disease continues to develop
5. Maintain proper plant spacing — overcrowded canopies retain moisture and favor infection
6. Clean threshing equipment between fields to prevent carrying infected seed forward

**When:** Throughout the growing season but most damaging at tillering through grain filling (July–October). Severity increases in late-planted crops and under stress conditions (drought, nutrient deficiency).

**Caution:** Brown spot is often confused with blast. Correct identification matters because management differs. If unsure, take a sample to your local agriculture extension office for diagnosis. Carbendazim resistance has been reported in some areas — rotate with mancozeb or tricyclazole.

### Sheath Blight
**Symptoms:**
- Irregular, grey-green, water-soaked patches on leaf sheaths (the tubular base wrapping around the stem), starting near the water line
- Patches enlarge, turn greyish-white with dark brown margins, and may cover the entire sheath
- Affected tillers may fail to produce panicles or produce partially filled grains
- White powdery fungal growth (sclerotia) may be visible on infected sheaths under humid conditions
- Disease progresses upward from lower sheaths to upper leaves

**Cause:** Fungus *Rhizoctonia solani*. Soil-borne and residue-borne. Favored by high temperatures (28–32°C), high humidity, dense planting, and excessive nitrogen. More severe in continuously flooded fields with poor air circulation.

**What to do:**
1. Avoid excessive nitrogen and dense planting — wider spacing improves air movement
2. Practice field sanitation: remove stubble and volunteer rice after harvest; deep plough to bury residue
3. At first sign of infection, spray fungicide: validamycin (Validacin 3L) at 400 ml per acre, or hexaconazole (Contaf 5EC) at 200 ml per acre
4. For severe outbreaks, combine validamycin with mancozeb (Dithane M-45) at 400 g per acre
5. Repeat spray after 10–14 days if conditions remain favorable
6. Consider alternate wetting and drying (AWD) irrigation — intermittent drainage reduces sheath blight severity compared to continuous flooding

**When:** Primarily during the reproductive stage (panicle initiation through grain filling), typically August–October in Punjab. Risk peaks during warm, humid periods with dense canopy closure.

**Caution:** Sheath blight often goes unnoticed until significant damage has occurred because symptoms start at the base of the plant. Inspect lower sheaths by parting the canopy during routine scouting. Validamycin is specific to Rhizoctonia and does not control other fungal diseases — use targeted products for each disease.

## Pests

### Stem Borer (Yellow Stem Borer and Pink Stem Borer)
**Symptoms:**
- **Deadheart:** Young tillers wilt, turn yellow-brown, and die — the central leaf pulls out easily with a rotten smell. Caused by larvae boring into the stem of young plants (vegetative stage).
- **Whitehead:** At heading, panicles emerge white and empty because larvae have severed the stem internally, cutting off nutrient supply to the grain head. Whiteheads stand out clearly against green healthy panicles.
- Larvae bore inside stems (not visible externally); small holes may be visible on the stem near the water line with frass (excrement) around them.
- Yellow stem borer (*Scirpophaga incertulas*) is the dominant species in Pakistan; pink stem borer (*Sesamia inferens*) occurs in cooler areas and later in the season.

**What to do:**
1. Clip and destroy egg masses on leaf tips during nursery and early transplanting stages — eggs are laid in oval clusters covered with brown hairs
2. Install light traps at field boundaries during peak moth flight periods (evenings after rain) to reduce adult populations
3. Apply granular insecticide at transplanting as preventive measure: cartap hydrochloride (Padan 4G) at 10 kg per acre, broadcast into standing water
4. For active infestations (deadhearts exceeding 5% of hills), spray: chlorantraniliprole (Coragen 18.5SC) at 80 ml per acre, or fipronil (Regent 5SC) at 300 ml per acre
5. For whitehead prevention, spray at boot stage (just before panicle emergence): same products as above
6. After harvest, cut stubble close to ground level and flood or deep-plough the field to kill overwintering larvae in stubble

**When:** Two to three generations per season. First generation: 2–3 weeks after transplanting (deadheart phase). Second/third generation: booting through heading (whitehead phase). Moth flights often follow rain events. Monitor using light traps and pheromone traps to time sprays.

**Caution:** Stem borer damage is irreversible once deadheart or whitehead appears — prevention and early intervention are essential. Do not spray during peak flowering hours. Cartap hydrochloride is toxic to fish — do not apply near fishponds or aquaculture sites. Chlorantraniliprole has a shorter PHI (pre-harvest interval, typically 14 days) and is safer for beneficial insects than older chemistries. Rotate insecticide groups to delay resistance.

### Brown Plant Hopper (BPH)
**Symptoms:**
- Small (3–4 mm), brown-coloured hoppers cluster at the base of rice plants, just above the water line
- Plants turn yellow-orange starting from lower leaves, progressing upward — resembles drought or nutrient deficiency
- Severely infested plants wilt completely and die ("hopperburn") — entire patches of the field may dry out
- BPH excretes honeydew that promotes sooty mold growth on leaves and stems
- Damage is worst in densely planted, heavily nitrogen-fertilized fields with continuous flooding
- BPH also transmits rice grassy stunt virus and rice ragged stunt virus

**What to do:**
1. Monitor fields weekly from tillering onward — part the canopy and check plant bases for hopper clusters
2. Economic threshold: 5–10 hoppers per hill at tillering; 10–20 per hill at booting. Below threshold, natural enemies (spiders, mirid bugs, egg parasitoids) often provide adequate control
3. Drain the field temporarily (2–3 days without standing water) — BPH cannot survive well without standing water, while rice tolerates short dry periods
4. Reduce nitrogen if infestation is building — lush, nitrogen-rich plants attract BPH
5. If threshold is exceeded, spray insecticide: imidacloprid (Confidor 200SL) at 100 ml per acre, or thiamethoxam (Actara 25WG) at 50 g per acre, or dinotefuran (Osheen 20SG) at 100 g per acre
6. For severe outbreaks, use pymetrozine (Chess 50WG) at 100 g per acre — specifically targets planthoppers with minimal impact on natural enemies
7. Spray directed at the base of plants where BPH clusters; ensure thorough coverage of lower stems

**When:** Typically builds up from mid-tillering through grain filling (July–October in Punjab, June–September in Sindh). Population explosions often occur after prolonged continuous flooding combined with warm, humid weather. Late-planted crops are at higher risk.

**Caution:** BPH develops insecticide resistance rapidly. Never rely on a single chemical class — rotate between neonicotinoids, pymetrozine, and other modes of action. Avoid pyrethroids (e.g., cypermethrin, deltamethrin) for BPH — they kill natural enemies and trigger resurgence (populations bounce back worse than before). Imidacloprid is harmful to bees — do not spray during flowering or near apiaries.

### Rice Leaf Folder
**Symptoms:**
- Leaves are folded lengthwise and stitched together with silk threads by caterpillars feeding inside
- Folded leaves show transparent white streaks where caterpillars have scraped away the green tissue (window-pane effect)
- Severely affected fields look whitish and scorched from a distance
- Caterpillars are green, 15–20 mm long, and active inside the folded leaf
- Damage reduces photosynthetic area and weakens plants, especially during tillering and panicle development

**Cause:** Caterpillar of the moth *Cnaphalocrocis medinalis* and related species. Multiple overlapping generations per season. Favored by high humidity, moderate temperatures (25–30°C), and lush vegetative growth from excess nitrogen.

**What to do:**
1. Scout fields weekly by examining 20–30 random hills; count folded leaves
2. Economic threshold: 10% damaged leaves at vegetative stage; 5% at reproductive stage
3. Cultural control: reduce nitrogen if damage is building; avoid late planting which increases overlap with peak moth flights
4. Biological control: conserve natural enemies (spiders, parasitic wasps) — avoid unnecessary broad-spectrum sprays
5. If threshold is exceeded, spray: chlorantraniliprole (Coragen 18.5SC) at 80 ml per acre, or cartap hydrochloride (Padan 4SP) at 400 g per acre, or spinetoram (Delegate 120SC) at 100 ml per acre
6. For light infestations, manually collect and destroy folded leaves in small plots

**When:** Present throughout the growing season with peak activity during July–September. Multiple generations mean damage can appear at any stage, but reproductive-stage damage is most consequential.

**Caution:** Leaf folder damage looks alarming but rice can compensate for moderate defoliation during the vegetative stage. Do not spray reflexively — check thresholds first. Chlorantraniliprole controls both leaf folder and stem borer simultaneously, making it efficient when both pests are present. Avoid spraying during peak flowering.

### Paddy Bug (Rice Ear Bug)
**Symptoms:**
- Slender, green bugs (15–20 mm long) with distinctive elongated bodies cluster on developing panicles
- Both nymphs and adults suck sap from milky-stage grains, causing grains to become empty, chaffy, or discolored
- Affected panicles have a mix of filled and unfilled grains; unfilled grains may show brown staining at the feeding point
- Bugs emit a foul odor when disturbed (hence sometimes called "stink bug")
- Damage is often patchy within the field, concentrated near field edges and adjacent to grassy weeds

**Cause:** *Leptocorisa acuta* and *Leptocorisa varicornis*. Adults migrate into rice fields from surrounding grasses and weeds at the milky/dough stage of grain development. They are attracted to fields with weedy borders and late-maturing crops surrounded by harvested fields.

**What to do:**
1. Keep field borders, bunds, and nearby ditches free of grassy weeds — these are breeding hosts
2. Scout panicles daily during the milky stage; economic threshold is 5 bugs per 20 panicles
3. Net and destroy bugs manually in small fields or localized patches (bugs are sluggish in early morning)
4. If threshold is exceeded, spray insecticide: lambda-cyhalothrin (Karate 2.5EC) at 200 ml per acre, or malathion (Malathion 57EC) at 500 ml per acre
5. Spray in the early morning or late evening when bugs are less active and clustered on panicles
6. Coordinate with neighboring farmers — paddy bugs move between fields, so area-wide management is more effective

**When:** Attacks exclusively during the milky to dough stage of grain development (approximately 10–20 days after heading). In Basmati areas of Punjab, this typically falls in September–October. Coarse rice in Sindh: August–September.

**Caution:** Paddy bug damage is confined to a narrow window (milky stage). Spraying before or after this window is wasteful. Lambda-cyhalothrin is a pyrethroid — avoid repeated use as it kills beneficial predators and may cause secondary pest outbreaks. Malathion has a strong odor; wear full protective gear including respirator. Observe PHI strictly — paddy bug sprays occur close to harvest.

## Common Problems

### Lodging (Plants Falling Over)
**Causes:** Excessive nitrogen application, tall or weak-stemmed varieties, high plant density, heavy rain or wind during grain filling, potassium deficiency, and deep standing water that weakens root anchorage.

**What to do:**
1. Follow recommended nitrogen rates and always split applications — never apply the full dose at once
2. Ensure adequate potassium (SOP) — potassium strengthens cell walls and stem rigidity
3. Use semi-dwarf or lodging-resistant varieties appropriate for your region
4. Maintain proper plant spacing — overcrowded crops have thin, weak stems
5. Avoid irrigation during windy or stormy weather forecasts
6. Practice AWD irrigation during vegetative stage to promote stronger root systems
7. If partial lodging occurs, drain the field to firm the soil; plants may partially recover if grain is still filling

### Empty Grains (Chaffy Panicles)
**Causes:** Water stress during flowering, extreme heat (>35°C) or cold (<20°C) at anthesis, excessive nitrogen causing delayed and uneven flowering, neck blast infection, stem borer whitehead, or varietal sensitivity to temperature.

**What to do:**
1. Ensure continuous flooding during panicle initiation through flowering — this is non-negotiable
2. Time transplanting so that flowering does not coincide with expected heat waves or cold snaps
3. Split nitrogen properly — excessive late nitrogen delays flowering and creates mixed-age panicles
4. Manage neck blast and stem borer proactively (see disease and pest sections above)
5. Choose varieties matched to your region's temperature profile during the expected flowering window

### Zinc Deficiency (Khaira Disease)
**Symptoms:**
- Bronzing or rusty-brown discoloration of lower leaves, starting 2–4 weeks after transplanting
- Stunted growth with reduced tillering — plants remain small and bushy
- New leaves may emerge narrow, pale, and fail to open fully
- Root systems are poorly developed and brown instead of white
- Symptoms appear in patches corresponding to soil variability — not uniform across the field

**Cause:** Zinc becomes unavailable in flooded, alkaline soils (pH > 7.5) due to chemical fixation. This is extremely common in Punjab rice soils where calcareous parent material and high pH prevail. Continuous rice-wheat rotation without zinc supplementation depletes available zinc over time.

**What to do:**
1. Apply zinc sulphate (33% Zn) at 10–12 kg per acre to puddled soil before transplanting — this is the standard preventive measure
2. If deficiency symptoms appear mid-season, foliar spray zinc sulphate at 0.5% solution (5 g zinc sulphate per litre water); apply 2–3 sprays at 7-day intervals
3. For severely deficient fields, increase basal zinc to 15–20 kg per acre
4. Avoid applying zinc and phosphorus fertilizers simultaneously in the same band — they can interact and reduce zinc availability. Space applications apart or use chelated zinc
5. Get a soil test done — zinc deficiency often co-occurs with other micronutrient issues

**When:** Symptoms typically appear 2–4 weeks after transplanting (during active tillering). Earlier appearance indicates severe deficiency requiring immediate correction.

## Harvesting

### Timing
- Harvest Basmati varieties when 80–85% of grains on the panicle have turned straw-yellow and grains are hard (not milky when pressed between fingernails)
- Harvest coarse varieties when 90% of grains are mature
- Typical harvest windows: Basmati in Punjab October–November; coarse rice in Sindh August–September
- Delaying harvest beyond full maturity increases shattering losses (grains fall off the panicle), especially in Basmati which shatters readily

### Moisture Content
- Ideal harvest moisture: 20–22% for mechanical threshing; 18–20% for manual harvesting
- Grain above 24% moisture bruises during threshing, producing broken rice and reducing milling recovery
- Grain below 16% at harvest shatters excessively during cutting and handling

### Drying
1. Spread freshly harvested paddy in thin layers (5–10 cm) on clean, dry concrete floors or tarpaulins
2. Turn the paddy every 2–3 hours during sun drying for uniform moisture reduction
3. Target drying to 12–14% moisture for safe storage — grain should be hard and make a sharp clicking sound when bitten
4. Avoid drying on bare soil — contamination with stones, mud, and weed seeds reduces milling quality
5. Mechanical dryers (flatbed or batch dryers) are preferable where available — they produce more uniform drying and reduce cracking
6. Do not dry paddy in direct midday sun (>40°C) — rapid surface drying causes kernel cracking. Morning and late afternoon sun, or shade drying with good airflow, produces better milling quality

### Storage
- Store dried paddy at moisture below 13% in clean, dry, well-ventilated godowns
- Use phosphine fumigation (aluminium phosphide tablets) for stored grain protection — follow label rates and seal the store for the required exposure period
- Check stored grain monthly for moisture buildup, insect activity, and heating

## Safety Warnings

**Pesticide safety applies to every chemical mentioned in this guide:**
- Always read and follow the product label — the label is the law
- Wear protective clothing: long sleeves, trousers, rubber gloves, boots, mask/respirator, and eye protection when mixing and spraying
- Never eat, drink, smoke, or touch your face while handling pesticides
- Mix chemicals outdoors or in well-ventilated areas; never in enclosed spaces
- Triple-rinse empty containers and puncture them to prevent reuse; dispose according to local regulations
- Observe the pre-harvest interval (PHI) printed on every product label — do not harvest before the stated number of days after last spray
- Store pesticides in locked cabinets away from children, livestock, food, and animal feed
- In case of poisoning, seek medical help immediately and take the product label to the hospital
- Do not wash spray equipment near wells, ponds, streams, or fishponds
- Protect pollinators: avoid spraying during flowering and never spray near beehives

**This guide provides general recommendations. Always consult your local agriculture extension officer or crop advisor for variety-specific, location-specific, and season-specific advice. Soil tests, disease diagnostics, and pest identification services are available through provincial agriculture departments.**
