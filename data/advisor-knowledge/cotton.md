# Cotton — Complete Crop Guide for Pakistan

## Overview
Cotton is Pakistan's most important Kharif cash crop and the backbone of the country's textile industry, accounting for a significant share of export earnings. It is grown on approximately 2.5–3 million hectares, primarily in southern Punjab (Bahawalpur, Multan, Sahiwal, Vehari, Rahim Yar Khan) and upper Sindh (Sukkur, Khairpur, Ghotki, Sanghar). Major approved varieties include CKC-122, IUB-2013, FH-2015, BT-3701, Neelum-Bt, and BS-15. Farmers should always use certified seed from approved sources — unapproved or smuggled Bt cotton varieties have caused severe CLCV outbreaks in recent years.

## Crop Calendar

### Sowing
- **Optimal window:** May 1–June 30 for irrigated areas; April 15–May 15 for early sowing in Sindh
- **Late sowing:** Sowing after June 30 significantly reduces yield and increases pest pressure, especially whitefly and CLCV
- **Seed rate:** 6–8 kg per acre (delinted seed); 10–12 kg per acre if using fuzzy seed
- **Spacing:** Row-to-row 75 cm (2.5 feet), plant-to-plant 25–30 cm (9–12 inches)
- **Seed treatment:** Treat seed with imidacloprid (Gaucho) at 5 ml per kg seed to protect against early-season sucking pests

### Key Growth Stages
| Stage | Days After Sowing | Critical Management |
|---|---|---|
| Germination & emergence | 0–10 | Ensure adequate soil moisture; watch for cutworms |
| Seedling establishment | 10–30 | Thinning to maintain spacing; first weeding |
| Squaring (bud formation) | 30–55 | First irrigation critical; monitor for jassid and thrips |
| Flowering | 55–80 | Peak water demand; scout for bollworms daily |
| Boll development | 80–120 | Second peak pest period; monitor for whitefly and mealybug |
| Boll opening / Picking | 120–180 | Begin picking as bolls open; avoid rain damage |

Picking typically begins in September and continues through January, with peak picking in October–November.

### Irrigation Schedule
1. **First irrigation:** 25–30 days after sowing (squaring stage). Most critical — missing this causes square shedding and stunted growth.
2. **Second irrigation:** 45–50 days after sowing (early flowering)
3. **Third irrigation:** 65–70 days after sowing (peak flowering)
4. **Fourth irrigation:** 85–90 days after sowing (boll development)
5. **Fifth irrigation:** 105–110 days after sowing (late boll filling) — if needed based on soil moisture

Apply 50–75 mm (approximately 2–3 acre-inches) per irrigation. Cotton is sensitive to both water stress and waterlogging. Avoid standing water in the field — drain excess within 24 hours. Reduce irrigation frequency during monsoon rains (July–August). Stop irrigation 3–4 weeks before final picking to promote boll opening.

### Fertilizer Schedule
| Nutrient | Total Dose | Basal (at sowing) | Top Dress |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nitrogen (N) | 120–150 kg/ha | Half (60–75 kg) | Half split: one-third at squaring, two-thirds at flowering |
| Phosphorus (P₂O₅) | 60–90 kg/ha | Full (all at sowing) | — |
| Potash (K₂O) | 60–90 kg/ha | Full (all at sowing) | — |

**Recommended fertilizers per acre:**
- DAP: 1 bag (50 kg) at sowing
- Urea: 2 bags (100 kg) — half at sowing, remaining split between squaring (30–35 days) and flowering (55–60 days)
- SOP (Sulphate of Potash): 1 bag (50 kg) at sowing — cotton is a heavy potash feeder; do not skip

**Micronutrients:** Apply zinc sulphate (25 kg/ha) and boron (borax 5–10 kg/ha) at sowing if soil test indicates deficiency. Boron deficiency is common in cotton-growing areas of Punjab and causes poor boll set.

**Important:** Do not apply excessive nitrogen — it promotes vegetative growth, delays maturity, and makes the crop more attractive to sucking pests. Always base fertilizer rates on a soil test when possible.

## Diseases

### Cotton Leaf Curl Virus (CLCV) — MOST IMPORTANT
**Symptoms:**
- Leaves curl upward along margins, become thickened and brittle
- Veins swell and darken; small vein-like outgrowths (enations) appear on the underside of leaves
- Plants are severely stunted; internodes shorten dramatically
- Flowers and bolls drop; infected plants may produce no harvestable cotton
- Symptoms appear 2–3 weeks after whitefly transmission and worsen over time
- Young plants infected before squaring suffer near-total loss

**Cause:** Begomovirus transmitted exclusively by whitefly (*Bemisia tabaci*). The virus persists in alternate hosts (weeds like *Croton*, *Xanthium*, tomato, chilli) between seasons. No chemical cures the virus once a plant is infected.

**What to do:**
1. Use only CLCV-resistant/tolerant approved varieties (CKC-122, IUB-2013, FH-2015, BT-3701) — never use unapproved seed
2. Destroy all weed hosts around the field and along water channels before sowing and throughout the season
3. Control whitefly aggressively from the seedling stage — see Whitefly section below
4. Rogue out (uproot and destroy) infected plants immediately when spotted — do not leave them in the field
5. Maintain recommended plant spacing — overcrowded crops create humid conditions that favor whitefly multiplication
6. Avoid late sowing — crops sown after June 30 face much higher CLCV pressure
7. If more than 30% of plants show symptoms before flowering, consult your local extension officer about whether to replant

**When:** Highest risk June–September. Monitor fields every 3–5 days from seedling stage through boll development. Early detection and roguing are essential.

**Caution:** There is NO spray that kills the virus inside the plant. Insecticides kill the whitefly vector but do not cure infected plants. Spraying infected fields repeatedly without roguing wastes money and accelerates pesticide resistance. Always rotate insecticide groups to delay whitefly resistance.

### Bacterial Blight (Angular Leaf Spot)
**Symptoms:**
- Small, water-soaked angular spots on leaves, bounded by leaf veins
- Spots enlarge, turn brown, and may merge into large dead patches
- In severe cases, stems develop dark streaks and bolls show water-soaked lesions
- Premature defoliation leads to reduced boll size and yield

**Cause:** Bacterium *Xanthomonas citri* pv. *malvacearum*. Spreads through infected seed, rain splash, and contaminated tools. Favored by warm temperatures (25–35°C) with high humidity and rainfall.

**What to do:**
1. Use certified disease-free seed — this is the primary control measure
2. Treat seed with streptomycin sulphate (Agristrep) at 250 ppm (soak seed for 12 hours)
3. Remove and destroy infected plant debris after harvest — do not leave stubble in the field
4. If blight appears in the field, spray copper-based bactericide (Cupravit or Blue Copper) at 500 g per acre
5. Repeat spray after 10–15 days if rain continues
6. Rotate cotton with non-host crops (wheat, maize) to reduce inoculum buildup

**When:** Most common July–September during monsoon rains. Scout after heavy rainfall events.

**Caution:** Copper sprays can cause phytotoxicity in hot weather — apply in the evening. Do not mix copper fungicides with alkaline products.

### Boll Rot
**Symptoms:**
- Bolls turn black, soft, and rotten before opening
- Internal lint is discolored, stained, or destroyed
- A foul smell may be present in severely affected bolls
- Affected bolls fail to open properly or open with poor-quality lint

**Cause:** Complex of fungi including *Alternaria*, *Aspergillus*, *Fusarium*, and *Rhizopus*. Favored by prolonged wet weather, dense canopy, excessive nitrogen, and insect damage to bolls (insect wounds provide entry points).

**What to do:**
1. Maintain proper plant spacing — avoid overcrowding which creates humidity inside the canopy
2. Do not over-apply nitrogen — follow recommended doses strictly
3. Control boll-damaging insects (bollworms) promptly — their feeding wounds invite fungal infection
4. Apply preventive fungicide spray at boll development stage: mancozeb (Dithane M-45) at 600 g per acre or carbendazim (Bavistin) at 250 g per acre
5. Repeat after 15 days if wet weather persists
6. Harvest opened bolls promptly — do not leave mature bolls exposed to rain
7. Ensure good field drainage — waterlogged fields increase boll rot severity

**When:** August–October, especially during and after monsoon rains. Risk peaks when boll opening coincides with wet weather.

**Caution:** Fungicides are preventive, not curative — they cannot restore already rotted bolls. Time sprays before expected rainy periods. Wear protective clothing and respirator when handling fungicide powders.

## Pests

### American Bollworm (*Helicoverpa armigera*) — MOST DAMAGING
**Symptoms:**
- Round bore holes in squares (buds), flowers, and green bolls
- Larvae feed inside reproductive parts, destroying developing seeds and lint
- Damaged squares and small bolls drop off the plant
- Large larvae bore into mature bolls, leaving visible entry holes and frass (droppings)
- A single larva can destroy multiple bolls during its lifecycle

**What to do:**
1. Install pheromone traps (10–15 per acre) at squaring stage to monitor adult moth activity
2. Scout fields twice weekly from squaring through boll development — check 20 random plants per acre
3. Threshold: spray if 5% of plants show fresh bollworm damage OR 1 larva per 10 plants
4. For Bt cotton varieties: the built-in Bt toxin controls early-instar bollworms, but monitor for survival of larger larvae
5. If threshold is crossed, spray chlorantraniliprole (Coragen) at 60 ml per acre — effective against all larval stages
6. Alternative: spinosad (Tracer) at 80 ml per acre, or emamectin benzoate (Proclaim) at 80 g per acre
7. Rotate insecticide classes between sprays to prevent resistance — do NOT use the same chemistry repeatedly
8. Conserve natural enemies (trichogramma wasps, predatory beetles) by avoiding unnecessary broad-spectrum sprays

**When:** Peak activity July–September. Two to three generations overlap during the season. First generation targets squares; later generations target bolls.

**Caution:** Pyrethroid insecticides (e.g., cypermethrin, deltamethrin) have widespread resistance in Pakistani bollworm populations. Do not rely solely on pyrethroids. Never spray during peak bee activity (midday). Coragen and Tracer are safer for beneficial insects.

### Pink Bollworm (*Pectinophora gossypiella*)
**Symptoms:**
- "Rosette blooms" — flowers fail to open fully because larvae web petals together
- Larvae bore into green bolls and feed on seeds and lint inside
- Exit holes visible on mature bolls; lint quality severely degraded
- Infestation often goes unnoticed until bolls are cut open

**What to do:**
1. Follow strict post-harvest sanitation: destroy all crop residues, stalks, and unpicked bolls by December
2. Observe a closed season (no cotton cultivation) for at least 2 months between crops to break the lifecycle
3. Use Bt cotton varieties with pink bollworm-specific traits where available
4. Monitor with pheromone traps from flowering onwards
5. If infestation detected, spray emamectin benzoate (Proclaim) at 80 g per acre or fenpropathrin + triazophos combination
6. Deep plough after final harvest to expose pupae in soil

**When:** Late season (September–December). Becomes more problematic in ratoon cotton and late-maturing crops.

**Caution:** Pink bollworm has developed resistance to many insecticides across Pakistan. Cultural controls (sanitation, closed season) are more reliable long-term than chemical control alone.

### Whitefly (*Bemisia tabaci*) — CLCV VECTOR
**Symptoms:**
- Tiny white flying insects seen when plants are disturbed
- Nymphs and adults cluster on undersides of leaves, sucking sap
- Leaves yellow, curl, and become sticky with honeydew
- Black sooty mold grows on honeydew-covered surfaces
- Primary concern: transmits Cotton Leaf Curl Virus — even low populations can spread CLCV rapidly

**What to do:**
1. Begin monitoring from seedling stage — check undersides of leaves weekly
2. Threshold: spray if 5–8 adults per leaf OR any nymphs found on lower leaves during early growth
3. Systemic insecticides work best: acetamiprid (Mospilan) at 80 g per acre, or thiamethoxam (Actara) at 50 g per acre
4. Alternate with spiromesifen (Oberon) at 200 ml per acre for nymph control
5. Spray undersides of leaves thoroughly — whiteflies hide there
6. Spray in early morning or late evening when whiteflies are less active and coverage is better
7. Destroy alternate host weeds (especially *Croton congestus*, *Xanthium strumarium*) around fields continuously
8. Rotate insecticide groups EVERY spray — whitefly develops resistance faster than almost any other pest in Pakistan

**When:** Present throughout the cotton season. Populations explode June–September. Most dangerous during early crop stages when CLCV transmission causes maximum damage.

**Caution:** Whitefly resistance to neonicotinoids (Confidor, Actara) is now widespread in many cotton areas of Punjab. If one product stops working, switch to a different chemical class immediately. Do not tank-mix more than two insecticides targeting whitefly — it accelerates resistance without improving control. Protect bees: avoid spraying during bloom hours.

### Jassid / Leaf Hopper (*Amrasca biguttula biguttula*)
**Symptoms:**
- Leaf margins curl downward and turn reddish-brown ("hopper burn")
- Yellowing starts at leaf edges and progresses inward
- Severely affected leaves dry up and fall off
- Plants appear stunted with shortened internodes
- Damage resembles nutrient deficiency but is caused by toxic saliva injected during feeding

**What to do:**
1. Scout fields weekly from seedling stage through squaring
2. Threshold: spray if 1 jassid per leaf on average OR hopper burn visible on 10% of plants
3. Apply imidacloprid (Confidor 200SL) at 80 ml per acre, or acetamiprid (Mospilan) at 80 g per acre
4. For resistant populations: use fipronil (Regent) at 100 ml per acre
5. Maintain field hygiene — remove weeds that serve as alternate hosts
6. Ensure adequate irrigation — drought-stressed plants are more susceptible to jassid damage

**When:** Most damaging June–August. Early-season infestations on young plants cause the greatest yield loss.

**Caution:** Jassid damage is irreversible once leaves are burnt — prevention and early intervention are essential. Do not wait for widespread symptoms before acting.

### Mealybug (*Phenacoccus solenopsis*)
**Symptoms:**
- White, waxy, cottony masses on stems, leaf axils, petioles, and bolls
- Infested parts become deformed and stunted
- Heavy honeydew secretion leads to sooty mold covering leaves and bolls
- Plants look grey-white from wax coating; severely infested plants may die
- Mealybugs cluster at protected sites (leaf folds, under bracts) making spray coverage difficult

**What to do:**
1. Inspect fields carefully, especially in sheltered spots (under leaves, stem junctions, inside bracts)
2. Threshold: spray if mealybug colonies found on 10% of plants
3. Apply profenofos (Curacron) at 400 ml per acre, or buprofezin (Applaud) at 300 ml per acre for nymph control
4. For severe infestations: combine systemic insecticide (acetamiprid) with contact insecticide for thorough coverage
5. Add surfactant/wetting agent to spray solution — mealybug wax repels water-based sprays
6. High-volume spray (200+ liters per acre) is necessary to penetrate waxy coating and reach hidden colonies
7. Remove and destroy heavily infested plants if localized
8. Control ants in the field — ants protect mealybugs from natural enemies in exchange for honeydew

**When:** Emerged as a major pest since 2005. Peaks July–September. Often appears first near field boundaries and spreads inward.

**Caution:** Mealybugs are among the hardest cotton pests to control chemically due to their waxy coating and hidden locations. Single sprays rarely succeed — plan for repeat applications at 10–15 day intervals. Biological control (ladybird beetles, lacewings, parasitic wasps) is highly effective if preserved. Avoid broad-spectrum insecticides that kill these natural enemies.

## Common Problems

### Square and Boll Shedding
**Cause:** Multiple factors including water stress at squaring/flowering, excessive nitrogen causing vegetative overgrowth, high temperatures (>40°C) during flowering, pest damage (jassid, thrips, bollworm), nutrient deficiency (especially boron and potassium), and cloudy/rainy weather during pollination.

**What to do:**
1. Ensure timely irrigation at squaring and flowering — this is the most common fixable cause
2. Check fertilizer balance — reduce nitrogen if plants are overly vegetative; ensure adequate potassium and boron
3. Scout for pests, especially thrips in squares and bollworms in flowers
4. Foliar spray boric acid (0.2% solution, ~80 g per acre) at squaring and flowering to improve fruit retention
5. Avoid irrigation during extreme heat (>42°C) — irrigate in the evening instead
6. Some shedding (15–20%) is normal physiological adjustment — intervene only when shedding exceeds this range

### Excessive Vegetative Growth
**Cause:** Over-application of nitrogen, high plant density, insufficient sunlight (extended cloudy periods), or variety characteristics.

**What to do:**
1. Reduce or stop nitrogen top-dressing if plants are growing excessively tall with few squares
2. Apply mepiquat chloride (Pix) at 250–300 ml per acre as a plant growth regulator at early flowering to compact the canopy
3. Maintain recommended spacing — thin overcrowded plants
4. Ensure balanced fertilization — adequate phosphorus and potassium counterbalance nitrogen effects
5. Select compact or semi-compact varieties for next season if vegetative growth is a recurring problem

### Poor Boll Opening
**Cause:** Late-season moisture stress, premature cessation of irrigation, cool temperatures, varietal characteristic, or boll rot infection.

**What to do:**
1. Do not stop irrigation too early — maintain light irrigation until most bolls have opened
2. Apply ethephon (Ethrel) at 300–500 ml per acre as a boll opener when 40–50% of bolls are mature but not opening
3. Pick opened bolls regularly to signal the plant to continue opening remaining bolls
4. Address underlying boll rot if fungal infection is preventing opening

## Harvesting / Picking
- Begin picking when bolls open naturally — do not force-open green bolls
- Pick in 3–4 rounds at 15–20 day intervals as bolls mature progressively from bottom to top of the plant
- Pick in the morning after dew dries — wet cotton picks poorly and stores badly
- Separate good-quality clean cotton from stained, insect-damaged, or immature bolls at picking time — mixing reduces grade and price
- Keep picked cotton clean: use cloth sheets, avoid contact with soil, and store in clean containers
- Dry harvested cotton in shade before storage — sun-drying yellows the fiber
- Store cotton at moisture below 8% to prevent heating and fungal damage
- Sell graded cotton separately — clean, well-picked cotton commands a premium over mixed lots

**Grading basics:** Cotton is graded on staple length, color, trash content, and maturity. Clean, white, long-staple cotton from approved varieties fetches the best market price. Stained, immature, or pest-damaged cotton is discounted heavily.

## Local Measurements
- 1 maund (phutti/raw seed cotton) = 40 kg
- Typical seed cotton yield range: 15–25 maunds per acre (600–1,000 kg/acre) under irrigated conditions with good management; yields vary widely by variety, region, and pest/disease pressure
- 1 acre = 8 kanals = 160 marlas
- Ginning ratio: approximately 33–35% lint from seed cotton (1 maund phutti yields roughly 13–14 kg lint)

## Chemical Safety Warnings
- ALWAYS read the label before handling any pesticide or fertilizer
- Wear gloves, long sleeves, mask/respirator, and eye protection when mixing and spraying chemicals
- Never eat, drink, or smoke while handling pesticides
- Mix chemicals outdoors or in well-ventilated areas — never in enclosed spaces
- Dispose of empty containers safely: triple-rinse, puncture, and bury or return to dealer — never reuse for food or water storage
- Wash hands and face thoroughly after handling chemicals, even when wearing gloves
- Keep all chemicals locked away from children and livestock
- In case of accidental ingestion or skin exposure, seek medical help immediately and take the product label/container to the doctor
- Observe pre-harvest intervals (PHI) stated on labels — do not pick cotton before the PHI has elapsed after spraying
- Record every spray application (product name, dose, date, target pest) — this helps manage resistance and trace problems
